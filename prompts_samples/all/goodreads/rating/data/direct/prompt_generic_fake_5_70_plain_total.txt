The idea of this book is brilliant! The execution is...tedious. If you speak video game lingo, then this will be right up your alley, but I was looking for plot. In order for the plot to proceed, the author must STOP interrupting every 2-3 pages (I kid you not) to explain some new element of this new world his characters are a part of. OMG! Get to the story! This is especially frustrating when (I'm spitballing here) 60% of the details provided about the Oasis, Avatars, and the competition for a vast fortune (which is the catalyst for the story) are unnecessary to understanding the events. There just there...to be...there. To quote a cliche: less is more. 
 In this novel, two competing ideas are at play: a virtual world and of course, the real world, which is, predictably, shitty. Why wouldn't it be? The imagined world of the Oasis knows no limits and is subject only to one's imagination. 
 The onset of the book establishes that in this futuristic society, everything has gone to hell. There is no one looking out for "the little guy," so people enmesh themselves in the Oasis. The Oasis has anything and everything to make you FEEL like it's real...but it's not. The problem with this premise is that it's imbalanced; the author has a few throw-away lines at the end about the virtues of reality, but NOTHING about reality is made to seem as great as the Oasis. Yes, there's a love-interest and jacking off in the Oasis isn't as good as the real thing, but nonetheless, the amount of time spent on the development of personal relationships pales in comparison to the time and space the author spends outlining, describing, and carrying out the story in the surreal-and frankly-super cool world of the Oasis. 
 The author is obviously passionate about his topics (video games, the 80's)...and knowledgeable, but he's also obsessed with himself. He really likes to hear himself talk....er, uh...read himself write. Well...whatever it is...his lengthy and unnecessary detours were distracting and predicable. You don't need to source every 80's pop culture reference. The overabundance of 80's jargon, pop culture, and obscure detail led to me wonder if this was an authentic and organic passion. Like Shakespeare, I wondered, "the [author] doth protest too much, methinks." If the hubris expressed by the author's style wasn't enough, the main character, Wade, is a super-human video-gamer who comes from nothing and goes on to (spoiler-alert) win the billion dollar prize...which he promises to "use for good." Yawn. Are we done yet. Wade has no real weaknesses. Boring! Who wants to read about a protagonist who, by sheer lack of lazy writing, has little obstacles to overcome. And therein lies the problem with the text: because the author chose to spend such inordinate amounts of time and space detailing his gaming world...there is little room left over for plot or character development. 
 If technology is your primary obsession and people come in a distant second or third...this is probably the book for you.

A friend lent me this. "You'll like it. It's like what you write, only different." 
 With that, I began. 
 The book is very well-written; the political intrigue is some of the best I've ever read, and characterization is generally very credible and complete. Martin also does a nice job of balancing a large cast of characters and several locations in a way that does not require the reader to keep notes or a scoresheet. 
 Martin is (in)famous for his character-killing ways, and so I was cautious about forming too many attachments. Good thing, too. (Incidentally, I wonder if that's an obstacle for the author, if the reader knows to distance herself from the story?) Still, the plot twisted in a couple of wraps that I didn't see coming, and I was roused to anger more than once. 
 Not my all-time favorite high fantasy, but probably some of the best, technically speaking. The world is very well-realized (though I have a personal preference for shading it a bit more away from our real world inspirations, so that it's harder for me to recognize historic English events and other ethnic groups) and the characters very real. 
 I've decided that original recommendation -- "it's like what you write, only different" -- was a real compliment!

*****SPOILERS APLENTY.***** 
 Yes, I was disappointed by this novel, but not for the reasons that I expected to be. I had read several reviews, and heard a few more, before I got my copy, so I knew that Atticus was going to be brought down to earth in this novel. That wasn't a surprise. Katie Guest Rose Pryal articulates nicely the reasons why Atticus is a lackluster hero in this piece: http://katieroseguestpryal.com/2015/0... 
 I was disappointed in the pacing; the first 100 pages lagged. I was disappointed because this book needed a bit of editing - not a lot, to be sure, but some! - and it evidently did not receive any. I take no position on Ms. Lee's capacity to give consent to publish this book, nor on the multiple explanations for how this book came about. I can't know the truth there, so I'm not even trying. Selfishly, I'm grateful for even a little more to read by Harper Lee. Selfish, but I already said that. 
 I was disappointed because grown up Scout, now known as Jean Louise, had done precious little growing up. She ran away, she took a geographic cure, but that hasn't helped her introspection. Her second coming of age, if that is what you can call it, was heavy handed. Despite oblique dialogue with her uncle, Jean Louise did not wrestle with the changes in Maycomb, and I am not sure she ever wrestled with how those changes were reflected back up in New York. The closest she gets to sitting with the tensions the reality presents is in her visit to Calpurnia's house, where she realizes she is getting her "company manners," but that, to my mind, falls short. The complex interplay of race and class in the deep south doesn't get the treatment it deserves. 
 Harper Lee's prose remains delightful, and when she is good, she is oh so very good - she can do one-liners so well, but (perhaps because of the lack of editing?) it isn't enough to carry this book. Still, I'm glad to have had the opportunity to read it. I would love to see someone write a follow up, perhaps 20 years in the future, sort of in the style of fan fiction. That would be interesting.

Rating is actually 3.5, but GoodReads doesn't allow for half stars. 
 This is an abridged review. You can read the full version here on my blog. 
 This is a book with some seriously stellar qualities, studded with little `irk factors' that detract from the overall product. 
 On the stellar side, Rowling's characters are palpable, her world is vivid, and if her prose is accomplished but not outstanding, her storytelling is none the worse for it. I whizzed through this book, kept picking it up when I should have been doing useful things like making dinner, and found myself thinking about the characters - as real people - long after I'd put it down. 
 It's also to Rowling's great credit that she turns the seemingly mundane opening on a local council into a story that is both gripping and moving. She has a great ability to humanise issues. 
 The very "adult" world of Pagford is certainly a far cry from Hogwarts, and it doesn't only prove that Rowling can write for adults. If you were tempted by the old-world charm of Harry Potter to see her as a unworldly, The Casual Vacancy shows she's nothing of the sort. 
 Some of her characters swear like sailors. Some steal. They're on Facebook. They hack computers. They smoke. They truant. They shoot up. They have sex. Setting her child and adult books side by side, you realise there's very little Rowling doesn't know - and couldn't write - about. 
 The Casual Vacancy is many things, but "dull" is not one of them. In fact, its very "non-dullness" has a curious effect: it's the kind of book you don't realise has flaws until you put it down. 
 So it was that I was already halfway through, and enjoying it very much, when I first became aware of what I think is the book's chief problem. This is a very skilful book that was weakened by Rowling's evident desire to put so much into it. 
 At its core this is a book about a girl struggling for a better life against the shackles of poverty, drug dependence, bureaucracy and society's judgments. Into this mix Rowling throws bullying, rape, teenage angst, self-harm, theft, domestic violence, sexuality, infidelity, ageing, parenting, mental illness... and added to all this, no less than 17 different people from whose perspective we variously see the action. 
 The result is a tug o' war for the reader's attention - and affections. It doesn't make the novel less interesting, but it does lessen its impact. 
 Then there is the convenience of Barry Fairbrother. Other characters are constantly thinking to themselves 'gee it's a shame Barry's gone, he was great with [insert random problem here].' That there would only be one person, even in a town of Pagford's size, who was any good at anything, seemed contrived. The fact that he was good at everything, and the absence of redeeming qualities in the others, didn't make the characters unrealistic in isolation, but didn't ring entirely true when I considered them as a whole. 
 The result was a book I found enjoyable and engaging right to the final word, but which I failed to find as devastating as Rowling intended me to. The bleakness is felt, but not to your core. In Harry Potter, I bawled when Dumbledore died in Book 6, but while Rowling got sorrow from me at the end of this novel, she didn't draw actual tears, and I think after 500 pages she probably should have.
